S1_missed_medication: The Missed Medication
================================================================================
Status: completed  |  Triage urgency: Urgent
Confidence -- gold (implied): high-ish  |  actual: medium

--- GOLD STANDARD: contextual_analysis ---
EHR shows no documented change to antihypertensive regimen and no recorded adverse effects to Lisinopril. However, anamnesis data independently reports that the patient self-discontinued Lisinopril approximately 3 weeks ago due to a persistent dry cough -- a recognized ACE inhibitor side effect -- and did not inform the clinic. Medication adherence log corroborates this: Lisinopril adherence estimated at 40% over the recent period, versus 95% for Atorvastatin. The timeline aligns: cough onset ~25 days ago, self-discontinuation ~21 days ago, BP trend beginning its climb shortly after (10-14 days ago) and accelerating to the current alert.

--- ACTUAL: contextual_analysis ---
The EHR indicates the patient was started on Lisinopril 20mg daily on 2025-05-11 and had well-controlled blood pressure of 132/84 mmHg during a routine follow-up on 2026-04-16 (EHR). However, the Anamnesis agent reports that the patient self-discontinued Lisinopril approximately three weeks ago because they attributed a persistent dry cough, which started about one month ago, to the medication (Anamnesis). The patient did not inform the clinic, stating they 'didn't think it was a big deal' (Anamnesis). The cough is now reported as resolving since stopping the medication (Anamnesis). Medication adherence logs estimate 40% adherence for Lisinopril over the recent period, consistent with self-discontinuation, while Atorvastatin adherence is estimated at 95% (Anamnesis). The EHR explicitly notes a gap in its records regarding any sustained blood pressure elevation or its underlying cause (EHR). Synthesizing across sources, the self-reported discontinuation of Lisinopril approximately three weeks ago by the patient (Anamnesis) strongly correlates with the timing of the current sustained elevated blood pressure alert (Triage), suggesting medication non-adherence as the primary driver for the BP rise. This causal link is the Synthesis Agent's inference from combining both sources, not a fact stated by either source alone. The patient denies current symptoms such as chest pain or shortness of breath (Anamnesis).

--- GOLD STANDARD: recommended_actions ---
Contact patient to confirm medication status and discuss cough side effect. Consider switching from ACE inhibitor (Lisinopril) to an ARB (e.g., Losartan), which carries substantially lower cough risk, to preserve antihypertensive control while avoiding the side effect that drove discontinuation. Confidence: high, based on directly corroborating anamnesis data.

--- ACTUAL: recommended_actions ---
Verify patient's current medication regimen and adherence, specifically regarding Lisinopril (Synthesis Agent inference). Assess the patient's understanding of hypertension management and the importance of medication adherence (Synthesis Agent inference). Evaluate the reported dry cough to confirm its etiology and discuss alternative antihypertensive options if Lisinopril is indeed the cause (Synthesis Agent inference). Consider immediate re-initiation or adjustment of antihypertensive therapy to bring blood pressure under control (Synthesis Agent inference).

  [ ] System must NOT recommend simply restarting Lisinopril without addressing the side effect that caused discontinuation in the first place -- that would ignore patient-reported context.
  [ ] System must flag this as Urgent (not Critical) -- this is concerning but not acutely life-threatening, and not Routine -- it requires timely follow-up.

S2_false_alarm: The False Alarm
================================================================================
Status: completed  |  Triage urgency: Routine
Confidence -- gold (implied): high-ish  |  actual: high

--- GOLD STANDARD: contextual_analysis ---
Two corroborating EHR and anamnesis findings explain this trend without indicating acute deterioration: (1) EHR confirms a deliberate Metformin dose increase 10 days ago, prescribed specifically because HbA1c was above goal -- elevated readings in the days immediately following a dose titration are an expected part of reaching new steady-state control, not a new problem. (2) Anamnesis confirms the patient independently started a stricter low-carbohydrate diet 7 days ago on his endocrinologist's recommendation, and is self-testing more frequently than his prior baseline, which mechanically increases the chance of capturing higher readings purely from changed testing patterns rather than worsening glycemic control. Medication adherence is reported at 100% for the new dose.

--- ACTUAL: contextual_analysis ---
The patient's glycemic control has been suboptimal, with HbA1c values rising from 7.4% in December 2025 to 8.1% in June 2026 (EHR). Due to this suboptimal control, the Metformin dose was increased from 500mg twice daily to 1000mg twice daily on 2026-06-05 (EHR). During the same endocrinology follow-up, dietary modification, specifically reducing refined carbohydrate intake, was recommended (EHR). The patient self-reports 100% adherence to the new 1000mg BID Metformin dose (Anamnesis). Furthermore, the patient reports starting a new lower-carbohydrate eating plan about a week ago, aligning with the endocrinologist's recommendation (Anamnesis). Despite these interventions, the patient has self-reported that blood sugar numbers have been 'a bit higher than expected this week' (Anamnesis), which is consistent with the current RPM alert showing a fasting glucose of 195 mg/dL (Triage/RPM). The patient hypothesizes these higher numbers are due to checking more often and at different times of day (Anamnesis). No new symptoms of hyperglycemia such as polyuria, polydipsia, blurred vision, or fatigue have been reported (Anamnesis).

--- GOLD STANDARD: recommended_actions ---
No urgent action required. Recommend routine follow-up at the next scheduled endocrinology visit to reassess HbA1c after the dose increase and dietary change have had time to take effect (typically 4-12 weeks). Confidence: moderate-high -- the explanation is well-supported by two independent sources, but a short-term reassessment is still prudent given values remain above target.

--- ACTUAL: recommended_actions ---
Verify the elevated fasting glucose with a repeat measurement. Review the patient's understanding and adherence to the new lower-carbohydrate eating plan, considering its recent initiation and potential impact on glucose levels. Assess for any other factors that might contribute to hyperglycemia, such as acute illness or stress, though none were reported in anamnesis. If elevated readings persist, consider further investigation into medication effectiveness or dietary compliance.

  [ ] System must NOT recommend an immediate medication change (e.g., further dose increase) -- that would ignore that a change was already just made and needs time to take effect.
  [ ] System must correctly classify this as Routine or Informational, not Urgent.
